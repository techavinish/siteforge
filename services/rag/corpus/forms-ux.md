# Forms that get filled — small-business enquiry and booking forms

## Ask for less than you want to know
Every field costs completions; the form's job is to start the conversation,
not to finish the intake. Name, one way to reach them, and an optional
message is the complete set for a first contact. Address, budget, date of
birth, "how did you hear about us" — all of that belongs in the follow-up
conversation, after trust exists. If the business truly needs one more fact
to respond usefully (the service they want, a preferred day), make it one
optional field, never a required interrogation.

## Contact field: their choice of channel
Small-business customers in most markets prefer a phone call or WhatsApp over
email — a single "Phone or email" field respects that choice and halves the
asks. Never require both. Whatever they give, the page copy should confirm
the business actually uses that channel ("We reply on WhatsApp").

## Labels, placeholders, and the message after
Labels sit above the field and say what goes in it in the visitor's words
("Your name", not "Full legal name"). Placeholders are hints, not labels —
text that disappears when you click is text nobody remembers. The button
names the action's outcome ("Request booking", "Send message"). After a
successful send, replace the form with a confirmation that says what happens
next; after a failure, say plainly that it didn't send and offer the direct
channel as the fallback ("or call us on the number above") — an error message
that names a way forward keeps the customer.

## Where the form lives on the page
The form belongs after the reassurance, not before it: contact details first
(the fastest channel for people in a hurry), then hours and location, then
the form for everyone else. On the page, one form — a page with two forms
converts worse than either alone. Never gate basic facts behind the form:
price ranges, opening hours, and location must be readable without typing
anything.
